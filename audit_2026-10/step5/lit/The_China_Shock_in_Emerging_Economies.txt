          The China Shock in Emerging Economies:
        Unbundling the Electoral Consequences of Trade
                      Exposure in Brazil*
                                       Daniel B. Jones1
                                  Irene Menéndez González2
                                         Erica Owen3

                                      September 20, 2026




            How has the rise of China in the global economy shaped electoral outcomes in
        emerging economies? Unlike advanced economies, emerging economies face import
        competition and export opportunities, creating winners and losers. Trade exposure
        may also activate two competing political responses, economic voting and redistribu-
        tion demands, which either reinforce or offset each other depending on the incum-
        bent’s partisanship. Using a shift-share instrumental variable approach, we study how
        Chinese trade exposure has shaped Brazilian elections between 2002-2022. When
        the Workers’ Party (PT) is incumbent, import competition reduces PT support and
        increases support for the non-PT Left and invalid votes. When PT is in opposition,
        import competition increases PT support. These patterns suggest a combination of
        economic voting and redistribution demands, but their net effect depends on the in-
        cumbent. Export expansion reduced PT support in all elections. To understand trade
        politics, we must account for how reduced-form coefficients may conceal offsetting
        political effects.

1       Introduction
The rise of China as the world’s manufacturing powerhouse has reshaped politics across the globe.
A rich literature focused on the advanced economies has examined the economic and political
effects of import competition, including the backlash against globalization (for review, see Walter
2021).1 Developing and emerging markets have also been profoundly impacted by the rise of
China, but through fundamentally different exposure. Many, like Brazil, have experienced growth
in manufacturing imports, as in the advanced economies, and also an increase in primary sector
exports to China (Costa, Garred, and Pessoa 2016). This dual exposure has transformed trade and
labor markets (Chagas and Sant’Anna 2024; Dix-Carneiro and Kovak 2023). Yet direct evidence
of the political effects of the China shock specifically in the Global South remains limited, and
what evidence exists points to competing mechanisms rather than consensus.2
    In this research note, we examine the political economy of the “China shock” in emerging
markets, with a focus on Brazil. Brazil is a large democracy, with a regionally varied economy.
It underwent a significant trade liberalization process between 1990 and 1995; it then experienced
a major increase in exports to and imports from China beginning in 2001. Evidence suggests
that trade integration shapes political outcomes, including voter preferences (Cavalcante, Moreno-
Louzada, and Menezes Filho 2023; Iacoella, Justino, and Martorano 2020; Rettl 2025) and legis-
lators’ positions on international trade (Campello and Urdinez 2021). Brazil’s political-economic
structure, combined with data availability, makes it well suited to studying the political economy
of trade shocks in emerging markets, though the theoretical mechanisms apply more broadly.
    One potential mechanism linking trade exposure to election outcomes relates to left-right policy
preferences, whereby negative (positive) shocks are thought to push preferences leftward toward

   Another channel linking trade exposure to elections emphasizes voting as a retrospective eco-
nomic evaluation of the incumbent regardless of ideology.4 Cross-national evidence suggests that
trade’s effect on government approval depends on skill composition, with high-skill exports rais-
ing approval among skilled workers and high-skill imports lowering it, with the opposite true for
low-skill workers, creating a polarization effect (Aksoy, Guriev, and Treisman 2024). In Latin

comes tied to international forces like U.S. interest rates (Campello and Zucco Jr 2016). Yet to our
knowledge, no study directly tests an economic-voting model of the China shock in Brazil at the
aggregate level.
   We argue that a more complete understanding of the political economy of regional trade shocks
requires modeling both sides of the shock and the possibility of competing political responses at
once. Trade shocks may shape party preferences by altering demands for redistribution, while also
affecting electoral support for incumbents through economic voting. Thus, estimated effects re-
flect a net outcome whose meaning varies depending on the partisanship of the incumbent, rather
than a single mechanism. This approach addresses three features of existing work that so far com-
plicate the interpretation of trade exposure coefficients and their political implications: studies
commonly focus on a single political channel without considering that a shock can activate multi-
ple, potentially offsetting responses; imports and exports are typically modeled in isolation, even

   Empirically, we examine how regional trade exposure shaped support for PT in Brazilian pres-
idential elections from 2002 to 2022, using microregion-level shift-share measures of import and
export exposure. We first show that these shocks affect local labor markets as expected, then
estimate their effects on PT vote share separately for each election, allowing the identity of the
incumbent – PT for most of the period and the far right in 2022 – to structure our interpretation.5
We find evidence that the redistribution mechanism dominates with respect to exports, with exports
robustly linked to lower support for PT, regardless of election round or incumbency. The story for
imports is more nuanced; when PT is incumbent, the economic voting and redistribution pressures
appear to offset each other. In the second round, the effect is positive and statistically different
from zero only in 2022, when PT was not the incumbent, indicating support for both redistribution
demands and anti-incumbent voting. To unpack these mechanisms further, we show that in the first
round, there is an increase in support for non-PT Left parties linked to import exposure when PT
is incumbent. Additionally, we find evidence of economic voting in the form of disapproval votes:
import exposure leads to greater shares of invalid ballots in every year. Analysis of survey data
broadly supports the aggregate findings.
   This paper makes several contributions. Our theory and findings underscore the need to under-
stand and be careful to interpret the context and potential competing mechanisms. By unpacking
the mechanisms through which trade exposure shapes political responses, we bring more clarity to
political economy debates about how globalization drives politics. Additionally, our findings sug-
gest that unlike in advanced economies, exposure to import competition need not lead to increasing
support for the far right, nor need the Left benefit electorally from economic growth associated with
exports. They are thus relevant to the broader globalization backlash literature.

2      Trade, mass politics, and multiple political responses
In this section, we develop a framework for understanding how trade shocks affect political out-
comes, especially in emerging economies that face multiple types of exposure. Our central claim
is that the electoral consequences of trade shocks depend both on the nature of the shock–whether
it generates economic gains or losses–and on the multiple political responses such shocks can ac-
tivate. Because these responses may operate simultaneously and in opposing directions, estimated
effects should be interpreted as net outcomes rather than as evidence in favor of or in opposition to
a single underlying mechanism.


2.1     Economic consequences

First, to understand whether and how economic interests influence politics in an era of globaliza-
tion, it is important to develop a theory of trade that accounts for both winners and losers. This
is especially important in emerging markets, which have often experienced a double-sided trade
shock with China: increased imports of manufactured goods alongside commodity export booms.
The standard approach in the literature is to theorize about the effects of imports (and sometimes
exports) on workers and their communities (e.g. Autor, Dorn, and Hanson 2013). Communities
with greater employment in exposed industries will thus be more negatively (positively) affected
by import (export) exposure.
      Empirical evidence from emerging markets demonstrates that trade liberalization had impor-
tant labor market consequences. For Brazil in particular, the 1990-95 tariff cuts negatively im-
pacted employment (Dix-Carneiro and Kovak 2017), wages (Dix-Carneiro and Kovak 2019; Ko-
vak 2013), and formal and informal transitions between jobs (Dix-Carneiro, Soares, and Ulyssea
2018). Workers in regions that faced larger tariff declines experienced worse formal labor mar-
ket outcomes compared to workers in other regions. Additionally, liberalization was associated
with disruptions and growing wage inequality in Latin America, particularly affecting informal
and low-wage workers (Dix-Carneiro and Kovak 2023). Rising export demand from China after
its entry into the WTO, however, led to increased income and wages, lower unemployment and
lower informality (Cavalcante, Moreno-Louzada, and Menezes Filho 2023).
      We posit that communities exposed to greater import competition will be negatively impacted,

while those exposed to greater exports will benefit. As evidence of our mechanism, we document
that exports are linked to higher wages, while import exposure is linked to lower wages.


2.2     Political channels

How might such trade shocks shape political outcomes? Existing research suggests several path-
ways. We focus on economic channels that predict varied political responses regionally depending
on exposure to trade.6
      The first mechanism emphasizes policy demands and partisan preferences. Negative shocks can
increase support for redistribution or protection, leading voters to favor parties that advocate such
policies.7 Classic accounts of embedded liberalism argue that governments sustained globalization
by compensating those harmed through redistributive policies (Hibbs 1977; Ruggie 1982). Left
parties often championed this compromise, though they also sometimes promoted more protec-
tionist alternatives. More recent scholarship raises questions about the erosion of this arrangement
in advanced economies (e.g. Ballard-Rosa et al. 2021; Hays 2017) and highlights its limited fea-
sibility in emerging markets, where tariff revenue has declined and fiscal capacity is constrained
(Bastiaens and Rudra 2019).8 In such contexts, the partisan consequences of trade may depend on
whether redistribution is viewed as credible and salient (e.g. Holland 2018). Given that our empir-
ical case is Brazil, we focus on demands for redistribution as a more salient policy than potential
trade protection.9 Thus, those exposed to negative (positive) trade shocks are likely to demand
more (less) compensation and support parties accordingly.

   The second mechanism is economic voting, which expects voters to reward or punish incum-
bents based on economic performance. Canonical studies emphasize macroeconomic indicators
such as inflation and unemployment, but more recent work also suggests that voters respond to
the material conditions in their local labor markets.10 This logic applies directly to trade shocks:
communities that gain economically from trade exposure are expected to increase support for in-
cumbents, while communities that lose are expected to reduce support for the incumbent.11 Im-
portantly, such responses are consistent with several distinct channels, not only individual self-
interest. A shock can register directly, through a person’s own job or wages; locally, through a
weaker regional labor market and eroded public services that reach even those not personally dis-
placed; or sociotropically, through the hardship of friends, neighbors, and the broader community
(Ansolabehere, Meredith, and Snowberg 2014; Bisbee 2025; Frieden 2022; Scheve and Slaughter
2001).
   Instead of voting for the incumbent or opposition, especially under compulsory voting, accountability-
driven discontent has a third possible outlet. A citizen who wants to punish the incumbent but sees
no acceptable alternative among the parties on offer cannot simply stay home at low cost: ille-
gal abstention in Brazil carries a fine and, in most states, downstream administrative penalties.
Given this constraint, discontent is instead expressed through a spoiled or blank ballot (Katz and
Levin 2018). On this account, invalid voting is not demobilization but a substitute for switching
one’s vote: the same economic-voting logic redirected into ballot spoilage rather than suppressed.
We expect that increased import (export) exposure increases (decreases) invalid ballots (defined
as spoiled or blank ballots). Note that this is conceptually distinct from illegal abstention, which
more plausibly reflects a resource-based mechanism: a decline in local wages leaves individuals
with less capacity or motivation to participate at all, in line with evidence that economic dislocation
can reduce turnout through apathy rather than protest (Gonzalez-Rostani 2024). Though empirical
work on Latin America often pools both into a single “non-voting” or “disengagement” measure
(Cohen 2018; Lioy 2024), we treat invalid voting as an indicator of disapproval, and thus as an
extension of the economic-voting channel. Empirically, we distinguish between these two types of

economic voting and demands for redistribution will tend to push electoral behavior in opposite
directions. In contrast, when a right party governs, both mechanisms push electoral behavior in
the same direction. Therefore, regression coefficients capture the net or overall effect of trade
exposure.
   Empirically, this leads to several predictions in the context of Brazil. Regarding PT vote share,
when PT is incumbent, the economic voting and redistribution channels push against each other,
for both import and export exposure. This means that the aggregate effects are ex ante ambiguous.
However, when PT is not incumbent, imports (exports) should lead to greater (lower) PT share as
both mechanisms push in the same direction. Trade exposure also has implications for the support
of non-PT Left parties. This outcome is primarily influenced by demands for redistribution, and
we should expect imports (exports) to increase (decrease) demand for redistribution, and thus the
share of non-PT Left parties.12 Finally, trade exposure can also shape citizens’ decision to engage
in absenteeism in the form of invalid ballots, which can be viewed as a form of disapproval voting.
This operates primarily through economic voting; imports (exports) should increase (decrease)
invalid ballots, as it increases (decreases) the likelihood of disapproval votes.
   In summary, these mechanisms are not mutually exclusive: multiple political responses may
be activated by the same trade shock. As a result, the interpretation of empirical findings must
be context-dependent. Shocks to the local economy can generate political responses through in-
cumbent punishment or reward, as well as through shifts in partisan support driven by distributive
considerations. Our empirical design allows us to partially differentiate between economic vot-

3    Political context: Brazil
Brazil is an interesting and important case in which to examine the mass political economy effects
of trade shocks. Brazil is a multiparty presidential democracy composed of 26 states and over
5,500 municipalities, with a federal system that grants considerable autonomy to subnational gov-
ernments. Presidential elections are held every four years under a two-round runoff system: if no
candidate secures more than 50% of the vote in the first round, the top two contenders advance to a
second round. It is the largest democracy with compulsory voting for those aged 18-70 (Katz and
Levin 2018).
    During the period examined in this study, PT (Partido dos Trabalhadores) or the Workers’ Party
was either the incumbent or the primary opposition party. PT’s electoral breakthrough occurred
in 2002 when Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva – then widely viewed as a left-populist challenger – was
elected president. Notably, on trade, the 2002 PT manifesto emphasized export expansion, diver-
sification of trading partners, and greater integration with emerging economies, including China,
as well as measures to protect domestic production from import competition. Overall, Lula largely
continued the macroeconomic policies of his predecessor, while implementing a progressive set of

                                                  8
social policies. The PT advanced a redistributive agenda focused on poverty alleviation, through
flagship programs such as Bolsa Famı́lia, which lifted millions of Brazilians out of poverty and ex-
panded social protections across the country (Hunter and Power 2007; Samuels and Zucco 2018).
As a result, PT is the organizing feature of Brazilian politics: citizens hold strong pro- or anti-PT
views, and no other party elicits comparably strong opinions (Samuels and Zucco 2018).
   Importantly, Brazil experienced a series of major political and economic shocks during the
period covered by our study. In response to the 2008 global financial crisis, the PT government
shifted its economic policy stance, expanding public lending and increasing the state’s role in the
economy. In late 2014, Brazil entered its worst recession in recent memory,13 driven by multiple
factors, including the end of the commodity boom. Dilma Rousseff, elected in 2010 as Lula’s
successor and re-elected in a tightly contested race in 2014, was impeached in 2016 amid mounting
political pressure and allegations of fiscal misconduct. The period was also marked by numerous
corruption scandals involving the PT. Following Rousseff’s removal, her vice president Michel
Temer (MDB) assumed the presidency. Temer was deeply unpopular and barred from running in
2018 due to campaign finance violations. Lula, the PT’s standard bearer and a figure more popular
than the party he leads, was also ruled ineligible to run due to a 2017 corruption conviction. As a
result, a relatively unknown running mate, Fernando Haddad, former mayor of São Paulo, led the
PT ticket in 2018 and lost to Jair Bolsonaro, whose platform of social conservatism and nationalism
marked a rupture in the political system (Samuels, Mello, and Zucco 2024).14 In 2022, Lula, his
conviction later annulled, narrowly defeated Bolsonaro in the 2022 runoff, returning the PT to
power.15
   Given this sequence of shocks, we examine each election separately rather than pooling across
the period, benchmarking results against 2002. This approach also lets the identity of the incum-
bent (PT or otherwise) inform our expectations for how trade shocks should shape support for the
party.

4.1       Trade Exposure Measures

We construct measures of exposure to imports and exports similar to those used elsewhere in the
literature on the “China shock” (e.g. Autor, Dorn, and Hanson 2013) and, in particular, largely
follow the data construction of Costa, Garred, and Pessoa (2016). We consider exposure at the
microregion level; there are 558 microregions in Brazil, akin to commuting zones in the U.S.
context. We take 2000 as a baseline year, before global trade activity with China significantly
increased.16 We measure the industry composition of workers in each microregion in that base year.
The base year industrial composition dictates which microregions will be more or less impacted
by the rise of China in the global economy, based on the industries that saw heightened exports to
and imports from China over the subsequent period.

4.1.1     Outcome Measures and Control Variables

We rely on a number of outcome variables in our microregion-year-level analysis to capture the
different mechanisms outlined in Table 1. Our main outcome measures relate to presidential elec-
tions and are drawn from the Tribunal Superior Eleitoral (TSE).18 First, we examine the change
in the share of votes received by PT (out of valid votes) in the final round of each of the 2010,
2014, 2018, and 2022 presidential elections, measured at the microregion level relative to the 2002
election. We denote the change in year t relative to 2002 in microregion i as ∆P Tit .
      Next, in the first round, we examine changes in support for PT, as well as for the non-PT Left.
This allows us to better distinguish anti-incumbent voting from redistributive demands. A voter
who does not approve of the incumbent but wants redistribution can move their vote from PT to
non-PT Left parties in round 1. Both are measured as share of valid votes.19 We also construct the
share of invalid votes, combining spoiled and blank ballots, divided by total votes, as an additional
measure of anti-incumbent voting.
      We also use the Census data to construct a series of controls. Specifically, from the 2000 Census
microdata (restricted to ages 18-60), we calculate for each microregion: the share of workers in
agriculture, the share of workers in manufacturing, the share of workers in non-traded industries,
shares of workers by race/ethnic group, the average income, the share of workers with at least 11
years of education, and the total workforce count. Finally, we draw on the Census data also as
outcomes to estimate labor market impacts of our import and export exposure measures. See Table
A.2 for summary statistics.

We now turn to our key results, drawn from microregion-by-election-year election outcomes.
      Table 3 reports the change in PT vote share from the second round of the presidential elections.
We find that export exposure consistently reduces PT vote share across years. Relatively constant
effects of export exposure suggest that results reflect net effects in which redistributive responses
outweigh economic voting. Although trade shocks plausibly generate incumbent punishment or
reward, the observed patterns are more consistent with voters on balance responding through de-
mands for (less) redistribution than through retrospective evaluation of incumbents. A one standard
deviation increase in export exposure decreases PT share by between 2.0 and 3.4 percentage points
across the years.23
      The effect of imports is more nuanced.24 For years when PT is incumbent, the coefficient on
imports is not statistically different from zero, suggesting that countervailing pressures of redis-
tributive and anti-incumbent voting are at play. In 2022, when PT is in opposition, the coefficient
is positive and statistically significant, and the magnitude is double that of other years. The mag-
nitude of the effect in that year is such that a one standard deviation increase in import exposure
increases PT share by roughly 1.7 percentage points. We advance these connections to theory as
“suggestive,” because there are of course other potential differences between 2022 and earlier years
that could impact voters’ preferences and how they materialize in election results.
      Turning to first-round election results provides a way to examine competing anti-incumbent

6         Conclusions
This paper revisits the political consequences of the China shock in an emerging economy. We ex-
amine Brazil, a context in which trade integration with China has involved both import competition
and export expansion, and where the electoral implications of these joint shocks remain compara-
tively understudied. Our results show that allowing for multiple political channels rather than one
is essential for interpreting reduced-form coefficients that would otherwise aggregate offsetting
effects.
     The interaction between economic voting and redistributive politics is central to interpreting
these results, especially with respect to import exposure. When PT governs, these two mecha-
nisms push electoral behavior in opposing directions, muting aggregate effects; when a right-wing
incumbent governs, as in 2022, the mechanisms reinforce one another and PT gains support. Our
results drawing on other outcomes (e.g., first-round election results) support these patterns.
     Interestingly, the pattern of results we observe for export exposure is distinct from import ex-
posure in a way that was not anticipated by our theoretical framework. Where import exposure
appears to operate through both theoretical channels, our results are most consistent with export
exposure operating primarily through the demand for redistribution channel and not economic vot-
ing. One possible explanation is asymmetry in economic voting, with political responses primarily
to negative shocks. But, as already noted, there is existing evidence of rewarding incumbents for
positive economic outcomes in our setting (Campello and Zucco Jr 2016).28 A different possibil-
ity relates to some of our supplementary analysis, where we found that the (positive) economic
effects of export exposure are more uniform across groups, while the (negative) effects of import
exposure impact some groups more than others.29 Existing work from the U.S. setting documents
how political effects of trade exposure may hinge on which groups are most impacted (Baccini
    28
     There are of course a number of papers on whether economic voting has symmetric effects or
not; for instance, Park (2019) revisits the issue and finds weak support for asymmetry.
    29
         Appendix Tables B.5 and B.6.



                                                 20
and Weymouth 2021) or the relative rather than absolute impacts across groups (Jones, Owen, and
Sung 2026). That could provide one explanation for the stronger economic voting response to
import exposure than export exposure in our setting. A further possibility, consistent with work on
grievance and status threat, is that import competition – unlike export gains – is experienced as a
loss to be defended against rather than a gain to be rewarded, independent of its precise distribu-
tional footprint. We leave further exploration of these possibilities for future research.
   Taken together, our findings caution against interpreting trade exposure coefficients as evi-
dence of a single political mechanism. Instead, they highlight the importance of viewing electoral
responses to globalization as the aggregation of multiple, context-dependent political reactions
operating at the same time. And more broadly, our results highlight that extending China shock
frameworks to other emerging economies requires attention to institutional context: party systems,
redistributive capacity, and the structure of trade exposure itself.

B.1   Heterogeneity in exposure

In this section, we examine whether differential economic impacts across groups lead to differential
political impacts. We do this because we have argued that labor market impacts drive political
response. As additional evidence of this, we look within groups to show that those who are most
exposed to trade shocks also are more responsive politically at the individual level.
   First, we document heterogeneity in labor market outcomes. We show that while both male
and female workers are negatively affected by import exposure, male workers are more negatively
affected (Table B.6).
   Turning to political responses, we present coefficient plots that split the sample between various
groups. First, in Figure B.3, we find that across all four outcomes, men exposed to greater import
exposure are more likely to vote for PT in both rounds; more likely to support the non-PT Left in
the first round; and more likely to abstain/submit invalid ballot. For women, import exposure is
associated only with an increase in support for the non-PT Left in the first round. While men and
women are both negatively affected by imports, the effect is larger for men, and thus suggests the
more exposed are more responsive politically. Exports do not have a statistically significant effect
on political behavior for either group.
   Second, we show that there are differential income effects by racial group. Nonwhite workers
are more negatively affected than white workers, as shown in Table B.5. In Figure B.4 we show
that non-white workers exposed to imports are more likely to support PT in the second round and
the non-PT Left in the first round; white workers exposed to imports are also more likely to support
not PT left in first round or abstain/cast invalid ballot. For white workers, exports are associated
with a decrease in likelihood of supporting PT in the second round.
